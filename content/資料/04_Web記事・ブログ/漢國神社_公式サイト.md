# About shrines

Kango Shrine was founded in the first year of Empress Suiko's reign (593).

By imperial decree, Ōmiwa no Kimi Shiratsutsumi enshrined Ōmononushi no Mikoto, and later, in the first year of the Yōrō era (717), Fujiwara no Fuhito enshrined Ōnamuchi no Mikoto and Sukunahikona no Mikoto together.

It was formerly known as Kasugai Sagawa Sakaoka Shrine.

The main hall is a three-bay nagare-zukuri style building with a cypress bark roof, a Momoyama period structure, and a designated cultural property of Nara Prefecture.

Within the temple grounds is the Hayashi Shrine, which enshrines Hayashi Jōin, who came from China at the beginning of the Muromachi period and introduced manju (steamed buns) to Japan for the first time.

### Rin Shrine

The Shrine of Manju and Confectionery

Located within the grounds of Kangou Shrine in Nara, Rin Shrine is dedicated to Rin Jōin no Mikoto, who is traditionally credited with introducing manju (Japanese steamed buns) to Japan.

Originally from China, Rin Jōin settled in Nara in the 14th century and began making manju using techniques he had learned in his homeland. His sweet buns, filled with sweet bean paste, quickly became popular among the people of Nara.

According to tradition, his manju eventually gained recognition from the Ashikaga shogunate and were presented to the Imperial Court.

The Rin family's confectionery-making tradition continued for generations under the name Shiose, becoming an important part of Japan's confectionery history.

Rin Shrine is revered as Japan's only shrine dedicated specifically to the origins of manju. In 1978, Tajimamori no Mikoto, traditionally worshipped as a deity of confectionery, was also enshrined here.

Today, the shrine is cherished by confectioners, those involved in printing and publishing, and visitors from across Japan.

The shrine's annual festival is held on April 19, offering prayers of gratitude and prosperity.

"Nara Manju"

Those delicious-looking steamed buns, taken out from the fluffy steam rising from the steamer, are not something that have existed since ancient times.

It is said to have originated with "dim sum," one of the lifestyles introduced from China by Zen monks during the Kamakura period.

Dim sum refers to sweets eaten when you're tired; it's a type of snack.

Around that time in Nara, a young monk named Hayashi Jōin, who had naturalized from China, made a steamed dish by wrapping bean paste in wheat flour, which he had learned to make in his homeland.

They named it "manju" and began selling it. This is said to be the origin of manju in Japan.

This steamed bun suited the tastes of the people at the time and earned the name of Nara Manju.

It spread throughout the entire country, and today there is no remote area where you won't find a manju shop.

In China, there seems to have been a type of mantou (a type of sweet) used in ancestral rites and served at banquets since ancient times.

The character "曼" likely derives from the meaning of covering something with filling, and "頭" probably refers to the dish served first at a banquet. (Shuowen Jiezi) Meat is wrapped in noodles and steamed. This is called mantou. (Shogakuki)

Thus, the name Mantou has existed since ancient times. Man means supreme or the best, and Tou means first, the best, or the first.

In our country, in a book called "Kumo no Itomaki" (Spider's Thread Winding), there is a section describing the customs of Edo during the Tenmei era,

The text states, "Manju, yokan, and senbei are considered the finest," placing manju at the top of the list of sweets.
